# Colorado municipal worksite wage-tax closure

Research date: August 26, 2026  
Disposition: `NO_AUTHORITY_CONFIRMED`  
Registry rows added: 0

## Scope

This iteration applied the national registry's narrow definition: a city or city-equivalent percentage tax calculated from an employee's wages, earnings, compensation, or payroll because the employee works there, including nonresident commuters, with employer-withholding relevance.

It excludes resident-only taxes, employer-only payroll expense taxes, flat occupational or head taxes, county or special-district taxes, business profit and gross-receipts taxes, and sales or property taxes.

## Conclusion

Colorado municipalities lack authority to impose the included percentage or graduated employee wage tax. Colorado moves from `UNDETERMINED` to `NO_AUTHORITY_CONFIRMED` with zero registry rows.

## Controlling authority

Current Colorado Constitution article X, section 20(8)(a) expressly prohibits local district income taxes; section 20(2)(b) defines district to include local government. The Office of Legislative Legal Services' current drafting manual independently describes the prohibition as applying to any local-government income tax.

The constitutional rule also predates TABOR. *City & County of Denver v. Sweet*, 138 Colo. 41, 329 P.2d 441 (1958), held that article X, section 17 vests exclusive, nondelegable income-tax power in the General Assembly and denies even a home-rule city authority to enact an income tax. *City & County of Denver v. Duffy Storage & Moving Co.*, 168 Colo. 91, 450 P.2d 339 (1969), reaffirmed *Sweet* and invalidated Denver's percentage earnings tax.

## Wage-based tax versus flat occupational tax

*Duffy* separately upheld fixed-dollar occupational privilege taxes. *Johnson v. City & County of Denver*, 186 Colo. 398, 527 P.2d 883 (1974), explains why: Denver's head tax was a uniform flat fee, bore no relation to income, and was not measured by salary except for an exemption threshold. *Rountree v. City & County of Denver*, 197 Colo. 497, 596 P.2d 739 (1979), later applied a special federal Buck Act definition but expressly left the Colorado-law rules intact.

Current municipal examples confirm that distinction. Glendale charges a $5 monthly employee fee, Greenwood Village $2 monthly, Sheridan $3 monthly, and Denver's code $5.75 monthly. They are employer-withheld worksite taxes, but they are flat amounts rather than percentage or graduated taxes calculated from employee compensation, so the registry deliberately excludes them.

## Product treatment

- Colorado coverage: `NO_AUTHORITY_CONFIRMED`

## Limitations

This is a current-law conclusion, not a permanent prediction. The municipal charter universe was not individually audited because the statewide constitutional rule and controlling cases eliminate the authority path for every municipality. The conclusion should be refreshed on the stated legal-change triggers.
