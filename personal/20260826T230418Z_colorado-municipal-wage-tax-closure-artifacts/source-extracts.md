# Colorado source extracts and legal classification

Research date: 2026-08-26

## Current constitution

The official 2026 Colorado Constitution is part of the 2026 Colorado Revised Statutes release. Article X, section 17 assigns the power to levy graduated or proportional income taxes to the General Assembly. Article X, section 20(2)(b) defines a TABOR district as the state or any local government, excluding enterprises. Article X, section 20(8)(a) then provides that no local district income tax may be imposed.

## Current official legislative interpretation

Section 9.7.2 of the Colorado Legislative Drafting Manual says that article X, section 20(8)(a) expressly prohibits any local-government income tax. It also states that Colorado courts had already interpreted article X, section 17 to prohibit local-government income taxes.

## Controlling Colorado Supreme Court decisions

### City & County of Denver v. Sweet, 138 Colo. 41, 329 P.2d 441 (1958)

The court held that article X, section 17 gives the General Assembly exclusive, nondelegable income-tax power and that Denver, including as a home-rule city and even with voter approval, lacked authority to impose a city income tax.

Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1958/18802.html

### City & County of Denver v. Duffy Storage & Moving Co., 168 Colo. 91, 450 P.2d 339 (1969)

The court adhered to *Sweet* and invalidated Denver's percentage earnings tax. It separately upheld fixed-dollar business and employee occupational privilege taxes, distinguishing those levies from the prohibited earnings tax.

Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1969/23940.html

### Johnson v. City & County of Denver, 186 Colo. 398, 527 P.2d 883 (1974)

The court again described the General Assembly's income-tax power as exclusive and nondelegable. It classified Denver's employee head tax as outside Colorado's income-tax prohibition because the levy was a uniform flat fee, bore no relation to income, and was not measured by salary except for an exemption threshold. The opinion said a tax measured by income would be an income tax and that an income tax must be graduated or proportional.

Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1974/c-491.html

### Rountree v. City & County of Denver, 197 Colo. 497, 596 P.2d 739 (1979)

The court treated Denver's flat occupational tax as an income tax only for the federal Buck Act's special definition. It expressly left intact the Colorado-law rules in *Sweet*, *Duffy*, and *Johnson*, including that a local tax measured by income would be a prohibited state-law income tax.

Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1979/27990.html

## Current flat occupational taxes

The current municipal examples found are excluded by the registry definition:

- Glendale: $5 per employee per month, matched by the employer, after a $750 monthly earnings threshold.
- Greenwood Village: $2 employee and $2 employer per month, after a $250 monthly earnings threshold.
- Sheridan: $3 employee and $3 employer per month.
- Denver's current code describes a fixed $5.75 monthly employee occupational privilege tax and a fixed $4 monthly business occupational privilege tax.

## Registry implication

The included product class is a percentage or graduated municipal tax calculated from employee wages, earnings, compensation, or payroll because the employee works there. Under the current constitution and controlling cases, such a levy is an income tax that a Colorado municipality cannot impose. Fixed-dollar occupational or head taxes fall outside the product definition and remain excluded.
