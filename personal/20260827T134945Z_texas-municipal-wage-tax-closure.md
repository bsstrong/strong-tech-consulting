# Texas municipal worksite wage-tax closure

## Scope

This iteration applied the national registry's narrow definition: a city or city-equivalent percentage tax calculated from an employee's wages, earnings, compensation, or payroll because the employee works there, including nonresident commuters, with employer-withholding relevance.

It excludes resident-only taxes, employer-only payroll expense taxes, flat occupational or head taxes, county or special-district taxes, business profit and gross-receipts taxes, and sales or property taxes.

## Conclusion

Texas municipalities cannot impose the in-scope tax. Under controlling Texas doctrine, a revenue tax imposed on a person for carrying on an occupation is an occupation tax even when its amount is measured by receipts or activity. Article VIII, section 1(f) of the Texas Constitution caps a municipal occupation tax at one-half of the state tax on the same profession or business. The Attorney General has applied that provision as a prohibition when Texas levies no state occupation tax on the pursuit. Texas imposes no state occupation tax on employment or wage earning, so the constitutional cap for the in-scope employee tax is zero.

Home-rule and general-law provisions do not reopen the path. Article XI, section 5 requires a home-rule charter to remain consistent with the Constitution and general laws. Tax Code sections 302.101 and 302.102 authorize municipal occupation taxes and charter-authorized tax collection only within those superior limits. Attorney General Letter Opinion 97-005 also states that municipal taxing power is limited to constitutional or statutory authority.

Texas therefore moves from `UNDETERMINED` to `NO_AUTHORITY_CONFIRMED`, with zero registry rows.

## Authority analysis

- Texas Constitution article VIII, section 1(f) limits every county, city, or town occupation tax to no more than one-half of the state occupation tax on the same profession or business.
- *Brown v. City of Galveston*, 75 S.W. 488 (Tex. 1903), treated that formula as a zero ceiling: because the state imposed no occupation tax on the pursuit, the municipal occupation tax was invalid.
- Attorney General Opinion JM-1195 (1990) expressly summarizes the rule as prohibiting a municipal occupation tax on a pursuit the state does not tax and applies it to invalidate a city occupation tax.
- *Conlen Grain & Milling Co. v. Texas Grain Sorghum Producers Board*, 519 S.W.2d 620 (Tex. 1975), defines an occupation tax as an excise imposed on a person for the privilege of carrying on a business, trade, or occupation. It explains that the taxable subject is the business or occupation rather than the formula used to calculate the charge.
- A percentage tax imposed on an employee because the employee performs services in the city is therefore an occupation tax under the controlling subject-of-tax test. Measuring it by wages does not create an independent municipal income-tax path.
- Texas Tax Code section 302.101 recognizes municipal occupation-tax authority, but it cannot override the constitutional ceiling. Section 302.102 similarly permits home-rule collection of charter- or law-authorized taxes, not taxes barred by the Constitution.
- Texas Constitution article XI, section 5 requires home-rule charters and ordinances to be consistent with the Constitution and general laws. A charter cannot authorize a wage-based employee occupation tax above the article VIII ceiling.

## Current-law and practice cross-check

- The current Texas Tax Code contains state occupation taxes for specifically named industries and activities, but no occupation tax on employment or wage earning.
- Tax Code section 101.008 separately prevents a local government from levying a Title 2 state occupation tax unless state law specifically permits it.
- The Texas Municipal League's November 2023 *Revenue Manual for Texas Cities* is a 111-page catalog of city revenue sources. It contains no employee wage, earnings, payroll-withholding, or local personal-income-tax chapter.
- The Comptroller's current city-tax overview identifies property and sales taxes and no employee wage-tax program. These administrative sources are corroboration; the classification rests on the constitutional zero-cap rule, not on silence from a tax list.

## Limitations

This is a legal-authority closure for the narrow employee worksite compensation tax, not an assertion that Texas cities lack all occupation, license, payroll-adjacent, or employment-related charges. Regulatory fees, fixed occupational charges, business taxes, and employer-side assessments require separate classification. The conclusion also depends on the in-scope levy being a revenue tax on the employee's privilege of working; a materially different legal incidence must be reviewed independently.
